For the last decade the advice was gospel: services are bad, products are good. Investors hated agencies because their growth was linear. They loved SaaS because its growth was exponential.

I believed it. I spent years trying to escape my own service business.

I was wrong — and not for the reason you'd expect. Services didn't fail to scale because services can't scale. They failed to scale because *I* couldn't scale them. And now, with AI collapsing the cost of delivery, the model I spent a decade apologising for has become the better one.

This is everything I've learned about scaling a service business, from founding division5 in May 2015 with no connections, no money, no references and no experience, to running it through the biggest shift our industry has seen.

## Part 1: The market doesn't care what you want to do

I wanted to build a game development company.

Soon after starting, we found ourselves without money. The only way to keep the business alive was to provide software development services. That was the first real lesson, and it arrived fast: **the market doesn't care about what you want to do.**

Most founders experience this as a defeat. You set out to build the thing you dreamed about, and the market hands you something less glamorous that actually pays. The instinct is to treat the paying work as a temporary detour — a bridge to the *real* business.

That instinct cost me years.

If you're reading this from inside a service business you fell into rather than chose, understand that you have something most product founders would trade for: customers, revenue, and direct daily contact with real problems. What you do with that is the entire game.

## Part 2: The three lies I told myself about services

I kept trying to pivot from services to product. All for the wrong reasons. Here are the three beliefs that drove it, and why each one is wrong.

### Lie #1: "Services are tightly coupled to the founder"

As division5 grew, I noticed our customers were more connected to me than to the business. I was doing the communication. I was solving their problems. I was making sure the service we provided was right.

I concluded services would always rely on me.

The truth is narrower and more useful: I hadn't set up a structure to decouple myself. In my defence, I couldn't — we didn't have the money. To build a structure you need to pay people, so I did most of the work myself.

But as we grew, I hired more people and built better systems. **The more systems I implemented, the less I was needed.**

Founder-coupling isn't a property of the services model. It's a stage. It's what a service business looks like before you've built the systems, and it feels permanent right up until you build them.

### Lie #2: "With services you don't build an asset"

The argument goes: your product is the outcome of work someone in your company does. If that person leaves, or that customer leaves, you're left with nothing.

What I failed to realise is that **the asset in a service business is the expertise, and expertise can be made structural.** You capture how the work is done. You encode it. You transfer it to new people when they join. Done properly, someone leaving doesn't gut you.

The asset was always available. I just wasn't building it, because I was busy trying to build a different business.

### Lie #3: "Services don't scale"

I heard the stories about product companies growing valuations overnight. I was having a hard time scaling. So I assumed the difficulty was inherent to my type of business.

**Every business scales. They just have different bottlenecks and different challenges.**

Often your business doesn't scale because of your inability to scale it — as it was in my case. Build the right systems, hire the right people, solve the right problems, and you will grow. Blaming the model is the most comfortable available explanation, which is exactly why it should be the last one you accept.

## Part 3: Don't cheat on your business

While I was building the product that would supposedly save us, I wasn't growing the actual business. You know — the one bringing in profit and solving customer problems.

I did that over and over again.

It taught me what is probably the most important lesson of my entrepreneurial career: **if you want to grow a big business, you need to focus.**

Don't follow the trend. Don't follow your passion. That cool idea you had yesterday? Drop it. Don't diversify. Keep doing what you do until you've improved every aspect of it. Stick with the service for an extended period and it will improve, and the business will grow. Changing direction means starting from zero again.

I call this cheating on your business because that's what it feels like in hindsight. The energy you spend on the exciting new thing is energy the paying business needed.

## Part 4: Growth comes from service quality, not marketing spend

For the first four years of division5, we acquired customers **solely through word of mouth.** We spent nothing on marketing or sales. We focused on the service we provided and the relationships we built.

Here's how that actually happened. A few months in, I was trying to figure out how to stay in business. Without connections it's brutal, especially when your clients are other businesses — most B2B collaboration happens inside existing networks. So I cherished every client I got, and did everything I could to deliver exceptional service and make them happy.

Profit wasn't my priority then. Maybe that was a mistake — I left a lot of money on the table. I wouldn't change a thing.

Some clients referred us. Slowly, we built a base that kept coming back, and they promoted us to their own circles until we had to hire just to keep up with demand.

We had accidentally built a service people love to share.

It's a slow way to grow, but it forces you to get the basics right. To be promoted you need happy customers. To have happy customers you need genuine quality. That constraint keeps you honest in a way a marketing budget never will.

## Part 5: Your service is your brand

As a young entrepreneur I was fascinated by Apple's brand — two people building a multi-billion-dollar company. So I read everything I could about building a great brand. Some articles stressed a memorable name. Others, the logo, the font.

Those things matter. One thing matters far more: **the experience your customers have with your service.**

There is a direct correlation between customer experience and brand. If the experience is good, your brand is good. If it's bad, your brand is bad. Think about Apple and "premium quality" comes to mind — because that's the experience people actually had, repeated over time until it hardened into an identity.

You cannot force a brand perception that isn't rooted in reality. A cool name won't hide that your service is poor. A well-designed logo won't cover for bad communication.

**The easiest way to build a good brand is to deliver the best service you can.**

And the first ingredient of great service is communication — not how articulately you speak, but how well you understand your customer. You're a great communicator when you're a great listener.

## Part 6: The systems are people, and culture is a system

Everything in Part 2 comes down to building systems. But in a service business the systems *are* people, which means culture stops being a soft topic and becomes operational infrastructure.

In mid-2020, right after lockdown, demand for our services surged. We were about 15 people and needed to hire fast.

Until then we'd never had a formal set of values. Culture wasn't something we'd intentionally built — it was something we lived. Under about ten people you shape culture directly, through your own behaviour. Past that, your personal influence dilutes faster than you notice, and you need values that operate without you in the room.

So I defined a set. And I got it wrong.

**I focused too much on the social side of culture and lost sight of the business side.** It took a couple of years heading in the wrong direction before I went back to the drawing board.

The correction was to build culture around what our customers actually valued — what made them come back — rather than around what made the office pleasant. An organisation's culture should make people feel good about their work *and* drive results. Those aren't in tension. Good results are a large part of why people feel good about their work.

We simplified to three principles:

- **Trust** — essential in both directions. If I don't trust you to do good work, we can't work together. If you don't trust me to make good decisions, you can't follow me.
- **Responsibility** — owning the outcome, not just the task.
- **Accountability** — the willingness to be measured against what you said you'd do.

None of these are unique to us. They're fundamentals that apply to almost any service business, which is precisely why they work: they're about the delivery of work, so they survive growth, remote teams and new hires who never met you in the early days.

The same logic extends to how you treat people, clients included. Business gets described as a shark tank; I disagree. **Kindness isn't weakness, it's leverage.** Clients are used to being treated as revenue streams and to vendors who hide behind contracts when things get difficult. Listening when they're stressed, owning a mistake before they find it, recognising that an angry email usually comes from someone under pressure from their own boss — that builds trust, and trust is the currency that actually scales. A satisfied client pays the invoice. A client who's experienced genuine care becomes an ambassador.

This is the part of a service business AI cannot touch, and it's worth being clear about that before the next section. Agents can carry the delivery load. They cannot be trusted by your client on your behalf.

## Part 7: What AI just changed

Everything above I learned the slow way, before AI. Now the ground has shifted, and most founders haven't noticed it moving.

### The old trade-off

Before AI the dichotomy was clean.

To scale a service business you hired people. Recruiting, training, retaining, the inevitable human friction. You constantly refined and measured processes just to hold quality steady while growing. **Services were easy to start and incredibly hard to scale.**

Product development was the opposite. Hard to build — capital, expensive engineering talent, real tolerance for risk. But once you had product-market fit, scaling was nearly frictionless. Serving 100 users cost about what serving 10,000 did.

That trade-off is why investors preferred products. It was rational.

### Services unchained

The bottleneck of human capital is dissolving.

You no longer need armies of junior analysts, copywriters or researchers to take on more clients. You need **agentic workflows** built to carry the growth instead.

Consider a marketing agency. In 2022, doubling your client base meant doubling account managers and creators. Today a lean team of three senior strategists can carry the load of fifty, with AI agents handling market research, data analysis, drafting and asset generation.

**A service business can now achieve software-like margins. Growth is decoupled from headcount.**

That is precisely the constraint that made investors distrust services for twenty years. It is gone.

### Products lost their moat

Meanwhile software products are worth less than they were two years ago.

Building software used to be the moat — the sheer difficulty of writing code kept competitors out. But if I can vibe-code your software over a weekend with current tools, why would I pay a monthly subscription?

Standard SaaS is entering hyper-commoditisation. If anyone can prompt a custom CRM or analytics dashboard into existence, how do you stand out? Positioning got exponentially harder. You're no longer competing against other development teams — you're competing against your own users' ability to generate bespoke solutions on demand. I think it gets worse from here.

## Part 8: The playbook now

**If you run a service business:** stop acting like a traditional agency and start acting like a systems architect. Productise your services — not by selling generic packages, but by building proprietary, agent-driven workflows that let you deliver premium, bespoke value at near-zero marginal cost.

Note how neatly this lands on the lessons from Part 2. Decouple the founder. Make expertise structural. Fix your own bottleneck. Those were always the answers; AI just made executing them dramatically cheaper. The founder who already built systems has a machine to plug agents into. The founder still doing everything personally has nothing to automate.

**If you build products:** features are no longer a moat and your code is not your advantage. Pivot toward what AI can't easily replicate — hard proprietary data, deep integrations with legacy offline systems, genuinely complex B2B workflows, or an undeniable community and brand.

**If you're building outside Silicon Valley,** as we are in Albania, this matters more. We don't have the luxury of burning millions in venture capital to discover whether our unit economics work. We need real, resilient businesses grounded in practicality, solving tangible problems. Cash flow is a better measure of success than a trendy office or a bloated title.

The good news is that the model suited to that constraint — a service business with strong fundamentals, real customers and disciplined systems — is exactly the model AI just made scalable.

The ecosystem doesn't need more rockstars. It needs more mechanics.

## What I'd tell my 23-year-old self

Stop trying to escape the business you have. The service isn't the consolation prize — it's the asset, provided you're willing to build the systems that turn it into one. The founder is the bottleneck, not the model. And focus beats every clever idea you'll have this year.

If I'd understood that in 2015, I'd have spent a decade compounding instead of a decade pivoting.

The old playbooks are dead. The founders who recognise the tables have flipped are the ones who'll capture the value in the next decade.

Adapt accordingly.
