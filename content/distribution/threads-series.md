# Threads series

The same eight ideas as the LinkedIn version, rewritten for a platform with a
500 character limit and a completely different register.

**Why the rewrite rather than a paste.** LinkedIn rewards a considered essay.
Threads rewards a first line that makes someone stop, and punishes anything that
reads like it was written for LinkedIn. The tell is a post that opens with
context before it opens with a claim.

**Cadence.** Threads rewards frequency far more than LinkedIn does. Five or six
a week is not too much, where on LinkedIn it would be. Post at the same time
daily, reply to everything for the first hour, and treat the replies as the
actual content rather than the overhead.

**Links.** Put the link in a reply to your own post, never in the post itself.
Reach drops on posts carrying an outbound link, and a self-reply keeps the main
post clean while the link still reaches everyone who opened the thread. That is
why the playbook link below always sits in the last part of a chain.

**Chains.** Where an idea needs more than 500 characters it becomes a chain, and
every part has to work alone. People land mid-chain constantly.

**One thing not to do.** Do not open with a question. Threads is saturated with
"What is the one thing holding your business back?" and it reads as engagement
bait, which is the opposite of what makes your writing land.

## 1. The bottleneck had my name on it

*Chain of 2. No link.*

**Post 1**
> For about six years I was the reason my company could not grow.
>
> Not because I was bad at the work. Because I was good at it, and everything
> queued behind me. Every proposal. Every hard client call. Every technical
> decision that mattered.
>
> I read that as being essential.

**Post 2**
> It was the opposite.
>
> A business where the founder is essential has a ceiling exactly as high as one
> person's calendar.
>
> The test is not how busy you are. Busy is easy to mistake for important.
>
> The test is what stops when you stop.

## 2. I started with nothing either

*Chain of 3. No link. Rebuilt from what he actually wrote, not a brain drain story.*

**Post 1**
> May 2015. I founded division5 at 23.
>
> No connections. No money. No references. No experience.
>
> The plan lasted until the money ran out, which did not take long.

**Post 2**
> The only way to keep the company alive was to sell services. So that is what
> we sold.
>
> Then demand outran what we could deliver and we had to hire. Which means
> recruiting, training, retaining, and tightening process constantly just to hold
> quality steady while you grow.

**Post 3**
> By fifteen people I had still never written down a set of values.
>
> That is the pattern I kept hitting for a decade. Whatever was blocking the
> company was almost always something that existed only in my head.
>
> Not a strategy problem. A writing it down problem.

## 3. Referral is a ceiling with good manners

*Chain of 3. Link in the final part.*

**Post 1**
> For years I thought a full pipeline meant the business was healthy.
>
> It meant the last two years had gone well. Different fact. Much less useful.

**Post 2**
> Referral arrives when it arrives. It reflects work you delivered nine months
> ago. You cannot turn it up in the quarter you need it, which is always the
> quarter you need it.
>
> Try this. Write down where your last ten clients came from. The actual source,
> not the category you would put in a deck.

**Post 3**
> Eight or more from referral and you do not have a growth problem yet.
>
> You have a growth risk. Which is worse, because it stays invisible right up
> until the quarter it is not.

## 4. I wrote our values and got them wrong

*Chain of 3. No link. Failure posts travel furthest here.*

**Post 1**
> Mid 2020. Demand jumped after lockdown, we were fifteen people, and we needed
> to hire fast.
>
> We had never written down a set of values. Under ten people you shape culture
> by how you behave. Past that, your influence thins out faster than you notice.

**Post 2**
> So I wrote some. And I got them wrong.
>
> I built them around the social side. What made the office pleasant.
>
> It took two years going the wrong direction before I started over.

**Post 3**
> The correction was building them around what clients actually valued instead.
>
> Those are not in tension, which is the part I had missed. Good results are a
> large part of why people feel good about their work.
>
> We landed on three. Trust, responsibility, accountability. None of them
> original, which is the point.

## 5. Kindness is not softness

*Single post. No link. This one is already the right length.*

> Business gets described as a shark tank. Eleven years in, I have never found
> that true.
>
> Clients are used to being treated as revenue. Used to suppliers who retreat
> behind the contract when something goes wrong.
>
> Owning a mistake before they find it costs one uncomfortable conversation.
>
> A satisfied client pays the invoice. A client who trusts you brings you the
> next three.

## 6. Five people became two

*Chain of 3. Link in the final part. Your most concrete number.*

**Post 1**
> Our recruitment used to take five people. An operations manager, two sales
> reps, a recruiter, one person in HR.
>
> It now takes two, and nothing falls through the gaps.

**Post 2**
> Everyone assumes we bought an AI tool.
>
> We did, eventually. It was the last step and the smallest one.
>
> First we wrote down how the work really happened, including the workarounds
> nobody admits to. Then we got the data into one clean place. Then we built the
> system the team actually works in.

**Post 3**
> Only then did we automate. By that point it was close to easy.
>
> Start at that last step and you get the pilot that demos beautifully and
> changes nothing.

## 7. The cost of a first draft went to zero

*Chain of 3. No link.*

**Post 1**
> One specific thing changed in the last two years, and most of the
> disappointment comes from misreading which thing.
>
> The cost of a competent first draft fell to roughly nothing.

**Post 2**
> Every process that starts with a blank page now starts much further along.
> Proposals, research, job specs, code scaffolding, first replies.
>
> What did not change is that somebody still has to decide the draft is right.
>
> Remove that person and you produce plausible nonsense at industrial speed.

**Post 3**
> So the judgement step is not a temporary safeguard until the tools improve.
>
> It is the job now. Worth paying more for than the drafting ever was.
>
> Odd conclusion for a tech company to reach. Hire fewer people, pay them
> better, give them tools that make their judgement go further.

## 8. What I would tell myself at 23

*Chain of 2. Link in the final part.*

**Post 1**
> Eleven years ago I left traditional education to build something. I failed
> considerably more often than I succeeded.
>
> If I could send one thing back it would not be a strategy.
>
> It would be this. The constraint is almost never the thing you are working on.
> It is the thing you have not written down.

**Post 2**
> Every time I was stuck, and I mean every time, something important existed
> only in my head. The business could not move faster than I could personally
> remember to act on it.
>
> Writing it down felt like admin. It felt like not the job.
>
> It was the job.

## What I would keep an eye on

Threads will give you far more reach than LinkedIn from a standing start, and a
worse audience for a €7,500 engagement. European service business owners are not
mostly there. That is a real trade rather than a reason not to do it.

Two things make it worth taking anyway. Reach you get is better than relevance
you do not, and these posts are already written for both, so cross-posting the
LinkedIn versions costs you a copy and paste.

Watch which platform actually sends people to /playbook. GA covers every page
now, so after four weeks the referral data will answer this properly instead of
either of us guessing.
