# LinkedIn series, eight posts

> **Threads is the primary channel now.** See `threads-series.md` for the same
> eight ideas rewritten as chains inside the 500 character limit. Keep this file:
> the two are the same material, so cross-posting here costs a copy and paste,
> and LinkedIn is where the buyers for a 7,500 engagement actually are even if
> the reach is worse.

Written to fix the actual constraint: the playbook, the pillar article and
/speaking all exist and roughly nobody walks past them. One LinkedIn visit in 28
days is the number this series is aimed at.

**Cadence.** Two a week for four weeks. Tuesday and Thursday morning, which is
when your buyers are at a desk. Do not post all eight in a fortnight and then
stop, because the point is that someone sees you three or four times before they
click anything.

**Links.** Only posts 3, 6 and 8 point at the playbook. A feed where every post
ends in a link reads as marketing and gets throttled by the algorithm and by
readers. The rest earn the right to the three that do ask.

**Voice notes.** Every one of these is true and sourced from the article, the
playbook or things you have said. No em dashes. Short paragraphs, because
LinkedIn truncates at about 200 characters and the first two lines decide
whether anyone opens the rest.

**Comments.** Reply to every comment for the first two hours. That is not
etiquette, it is distribution: replies are the strongest signal the feed has.

## 1. The bottleneck had my name on it

*No link. Opens the series with the admission, not the advice.*

> For about six years I was the reason my company could not grow.
>
> Not because I was bad at the work. Because I was good at it, and everything
> queued behind me. Every proposal, every difficult client call, every technical
> decision that mattered.
>
> I read that as being essential. It was the opposite. A business where the
> founder is essential is a business with a ceiling exactly as high as one
> person's calendar.
>
> The test I use now is not how busy you are. Busy is easy to mistake for
> important. The test is what stops when you stop.
>
> If you took two weeks off with no phone, what would still be sitting there
> when you got back?
>
> That list is not your workload. It is your constraint.

## 2. Everyone with a track record had already left

*No link. This is the Albania post. It is your strongest differentiator and most
people who follow you do not know it.*

> When I started hiring in Albania in 2015, the good developers were gone.
> Not busy. Gone. Berlin, London, Toronto.
>
> So I had a choice. Compete for the handful who stayed, or hire people with no
> track record at all and bring them up myself.
>
> I did the second one, mostly because I could not afford the first.
>
> It turned out to be the better decision, for a reason I did not see coming.
> When most of your team is learning, nothing can live in your head. Every
> judgement you make has to become something you can teach, or the company stops
> at the edge of your own attention.
>
> Founders usually hit that wall somewhere past fifty people. I hit it at ten.
> Then again at fifteen.
>
> I was forced to write down what most founders are allowed to leave implicit,
> a decade before I would have chosen to.

## 3. Referral is a ceiling with good manners

*Links to the playbook. First ask of the series, and only after two posts that
gave something away.*

> For years I thought a full pipeline meant the business was healthy.
>
> It meant the last two years had gone well. That is a different fact, and a much
> less useful one.
>
> Referral arrives when it arrives. It reflects work you delivered nine months
> ago. You cannot turn it up in the quarter you actually need it, which is always
> the quarter you need it.
>
> Here is the uncomfortable exercise. Write down where your last ten clients came
> from. Not the category you would put in a deck. The actual source.
>
> If eight or more are referral, you do not have a growth problem yet.
>
> You have a growth risk, which is worse, because it stays invisible right up
> until the quarter it is not.
>
> I put this and five other diagnostics in a short playbook, free, no sequence
> afterwards: engjellrraklli.com/playbook

## 4. I wrote our values and got them wrong

*No link. Failure posts outperform advice posts, and this one is documented.*

> Mid 2020, right after lockdown. Demand jumped, we were around fifteen people,
> and we needed to hire fast.
>
> We had never written down a set of values. Under about ten people you shape
> culture through your own behaviour and it works. Past that, your influence
> thins out faster than you notice.
>
> So I wrote some. And I got them wrong.
>
> I built them around the social side of the company. What made the office
> pleasant. It took me a couple of years going in the wrong direction before I
> started over and built them around what clients actually valued instead.
>
> Those two things are not in tension, which is the part I had missed. Good
> results are a large part of why people feel good about their work.
>
> We ended up with three. Trust, responsibility, accountability. None of them
> original, which is the point. They survive new hires and growth precisely
> because they are fundamentals.

## 5. Kindness is not softness

*No link. Shortest post in the series. Let it breathe.*

> Business gets described as a shark tank. Eleven years in, I have never found
> that true.
>
> Clients are used to being treated as revenue. They are used to suppliers who
> retreat behind the contract the moment something goes wrong.
>
> Owning a mistake before they find it costs you one uncomfortable conversation.
> It buys you something that compounds for years.
>
> A satisfied client pays the invoice.
>
> A client who trusts you brings you the next three.

## 6. Five people became two

*Links to the playbook. Your most concrete proof and the most screenshot-worthy
number you have.*

> Our recruitment used to take five people. An operations manager, two sales
> reps, a recruiter, and one person in HR.
>
> It now takes two, and nothing falls through the gaps.
>
> The part people assume is that we bought an AI tool. We did not, or at least
> that was the last step and the smallest one.
>
> What actually happened, in order. We wrote down how the work really happened,
> including the workarounds nobody admits to in a meeting. We got the data into
> one clean place so software could see a whole picture instead of one corner.
> We built the system the team actually works in.
>
> Then we automated, and by that point it was close to easy.
>
> That order is the whole thing. Start at step four and you get the pilot that
> demos beautifully and changes nothing.
>
> The full sequence is in the playbook: engjellrraklli.com/playbook

## 7. The cost of a first draft went to zero

*No link. The AI post, but with the argument nobody else is making.*

> One specific thing changed in the last two years, and most of the disappointment
> comes from misreading which thing.
>
> The cost of a competent first draft fell to roughly nothing. Every process that
> begins with a blank page now starts much further along. Proposals, research,
> job specs, code scaffolding, first replies.
>
> What did not change is that somebody still has to decide the draft is right.
>
> And they need enough context to know when a confident answer is wrong. Remove
> that person and you produce plausible nonsense at industrial speed.
>
> So the judgement step is not a temporary safeguard until the tools improve.
> It is the job now. It is worth paying more for than the drafting ever was.
>
> Which is an odd conclusion for a tech company to reach. Hire fewer people,
> pay them better, and give them tools that make their judgement go further.

## 8. What I would tell myself at 23

*Links to the playbook. Closes the loop with the article's own ending.*

> Eleven years ago I stepped away from traditional education to build something.
> I failed considerably more often than I succeeded.
>
> If I could send one thing back, it would not be a strategy. It would be this.
>
> The constraint is almost never the thing you are working on. It is the thing
> you have not written down.
>
> Every time I was stuck, and I mean every time, the answer was that something
> important existed only in my head, and the business could not move faster than
> I could personally remember to act on it.
>
> Writing it down felt like admin. It felt like not the job.
>
> It was the job.
>
> I put the six things I check for into a short playbook. Free, ten pages,
> written to be worked through rather than read: engjellrraklli.com/playbook
