Where This Comes From02 / 11

Everything here
cost me money
to learn

In May 2015 I founded division5 in Albania. I was 23, with no connections, no money, no references and no experience. Demand eventually outran what we could deliver, we had to hire, and every year after that the thing blocking the company turned out to be something that existed only in my head.

Thousands

of jobs created for young Albanians since then, in a country losing its best people to emigration faster than it can replace them.

A service business does not stall because the work is bad. It stalls because the work is different every time, so nothing compounds. Every new client resets you to the blank page, and the only thing that scales is the number of hours you personally sell.

Productizing is the fix, and it has an order. Most founders start at the end, buying software to automate a process nobody has written down. That makes the mess run faster. This is the order that works.

How to use it

Eight steps. Each ends with something to compile. Answer them in your head and you will flatter yourself through every one, so give it an afternoon and something to write on. The finished document is the point.

Step One03 / 11

What you
actually sell

Ask a founder what their company does and you get a category. Ask what they sold last month and you get a list of favours. The gap between those two answers is where the margin went.

Productizing does not start with inventing an offer. It starts with noticing which one you have already sold more than once, because that is the one the market has already voted for. Almost every service business has one and cannot see it, because repetition feels like the boring work rather than the valuable work.

The test: if your second client costs materially less to serve than your first, that offer is productized. If every engagement starts from a blank page, it is not.

Compile

List the three things you sold most often in the last twelve months. For each one, write the scope in a single sentence, and mark how much of the work was identical to the time before.

M mostly the same   H half the same   N new every time

Step Two04 / 11

Who pays,
and from where

Revenue tells you the business works. Where it comes from tells you whether it can grow. A company selling only into its own city has a ceiling set by that city, and no amount of process fixes that.

This step is uncomfortable because it is usually the first time the concentration becomes visible. Most service businesses discover that a small number of clients are most of the revenue, and that the pipeline that produced them was one person's network.

Neither is a disaster. Both are things you can only fix once you have written them down.

Compile

Your last ten customers. For each: country, what they bought, what they paid, and how they found you. Pull it from your records, not from memory.

Then two numbers. What share of revenue is your largest client? What share comes from outside your own country?

Step Three05 / 11

How the work
really happens

Every company has two processes. The one in the proposal, and the one people actually follow, which includes the workarounds nobody puts in writing because they feel like admissions.

The second one is the real asset. It is also the one that lives in people's heads, which is why the business cannot grow past the number of heads that hold it, and why every new hire takes six months to become useful.

Do not write the tidy version. The tidy version is what you already have, and it has never helped anyone deliver anything.

Compile

Take your most repeated offer and map it end to end: every step from first contact to final invoice. Mark each step with who does it and how long it takes.

Then mark every step that depends on one specific person knowing something that is written down nowhere. Those marks are your fragility, in order.

Step Four06 / 11

Where the work
comes from

Ask most service founders how they get clients and the honest answer is referrals and luck. Referrals are wonderful and they are not a channel, because you cannot turn them up when you need to. A business whose only source of work is other people remembering it exists is not in control of its own growth.

This is the step most founders skip, and it is the one that decides whether productizing was worth doing at all. A repeatable offer with an unrepeatable pipeline is just a tidier version of the same problem.

The question is not which channel is best. It is which one you have actual evidence for.

Compile

For the same ten customers, write where each one genuinely originated. Not the last touch. The first.

Then pick the single channel with the most evidence behind it and write one sentence on what doing three times as much of it would require.

Step Five07 / 11

What only
you can do

Every service business has one constraint everything else queues behind, and in most of them it is the founder. This is not a character flaw. It is what happens when a company grows out of one person being good at something.

The test is not how busy you are, because busy is easy to mistake for essential. The test is what stops when you stop.

Sorting the answer matters more than the answer. Work that needs your context can be documented. Work that needs your authority can be delegated the day you decide to. Only the third kind is genuinely yours, and it is always a much shorter list than it feels like at four in the afternoon.

Compile

For one week, log every decision that came to you, as it happens. Not from memory at the end.

Then mark each one:

C only you had the context   A only you had the authority   S only you had the skill

Step Six08 / 11

One clean version
of your numbers

By this point you have written down what you sell, who buys it, how it gets delivered and where it comes from. The next question is where each of those lives, and the answer is usually: four places, disagreeing.

This is the step that gates everything after it. You cannot automate a process whose data is scattered across a spreadsheet, an inbox, a project tool and somebody's memory, because the automation has nothing reliable to read. Every attempt to skip this produces software that is technically working and practically ignored.

One version of each thing. Customers, projects, money, people. Not one tool necessarily, but one authoritative source per thing, and everyone agreeing which it is.

Compile

Four rows: customers, projects, invoices, people. For each, write where the truth lives today, and who would notice first if it were wrong.

Any row where you named more than one place is a row that will break the next step.

Step Seven09 / 11

Only now,
automate

Six steps in, automation stops being a purchase and becomes an obvious next move. You know which offer repeats, how it is delivered, where the work originates and where the data lives. That is everything a system needs to be built around.

Start where the work begins with a blank page and ends with something predictable: a first draft, a summary, a categorisation, a routine reply. Keep a person on the judgement step permanently, and measure one thing, which is time to first usable draft.

What not to do: automate a process nobody has straightened out. It only makes the mess run faster, and it teaches the team that these tools do not work.

This is a chapter rather than a book because it is genuinely the last part, and because I have written the long version elsewhere.

Compile

Pick one process from step three that starts with a blank page. Write down the data it needs, and where each piece comes from according to step six.

If any of it is missing, that is the actual next task, not the automation.

Step Eight10 / 11

The next
ninety days

You now have eight sheets of paper describing your own company more honestly than any deck you have ever made. The temptation is to fix all of it. Resist that, because a plan with nine priorities has none.

One constraint. One process. One source of work that is not referral. Everything else waits, and waiting is a decision you make once rather than a thing that happens to you.

Ninety days is long enough to finish something and short enough that you cannot postpone starting. Put owners against each item, and make them people rather than departments.

Compile

Three lines, and no more than three.

The one constraint I am removing. The one process I am productizing. The one channel I am building. An owner and a date beside each.
